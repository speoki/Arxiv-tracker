Hypergraphs on Riemannian manifolds and their double homology
Authors: Shiquan Ren
First: 2026-08-01T15:10:42+00:00 · Latest: 2026-10-21T15:28:49+00:00
Comments: 60 pages; minor corrections

Abstract
In this paper, by constructing double homology for graded submanifolds of $Δ$-manifolds, we study the double homology of hyper(di)graphs on Riemannian manifolds. With the help of the canonical projection $π$ from ordered sequences to their underlying unordered sets, we prove a commutative diagram of the double homology and a commutative diagram of the spaces of differential forms of hyper(di)graphs on Riemannian manifolds. Moreover, we give some relations between the fundamental group(oid)s of hyper(di)graphs on surfaces and the (pure) braid groups on surfaces by certain homomorphisms induced from $π$. As applications, we study the homotopy types of filtrations for packings and coverings of balls on Riemannian manifolds with hypergraph constraints, and we characterize regular maps on Riemannian manifolds by geometric realizations of the independence complexes and hypergraphs on the manifolds.

Abstract
Real-world multivariate time series anomalies are rare and often unlabeled. Additionally, prevailing methods rely on increasingly complex architectures tuned to benchmarks, detecting only fragments of anomalous segments and overstating performance. In this paper, we introduce OracleAD, a simple and interpretable unsupervised framework for multivariate time series anomaly detection. OracleAD encodes each variable's past sequence into a single causal embedding to jointly predict the present time point and reconstruct the input window, effectively modeling temporal dynamics. These embeddings then undergo a self-attention mechanism to project them into a shared latent space and capture spatial relationships. These relationships are not static, since they are modeled by a property that emerges from each variable's temporal dynamics. The projected embeddings are aligned to a Stable Latent Structure (SLS) representing normal-state relationships. Anomalies are identified using a dual scoring mechanism based on prediction error and deviation from the SLS, enabling fine-grained anomaly diagnosis at each time point and across individual variables. Since any noticeable SLS deviation originates from embeddings that violate the learned temporal causality of normal data, OracleAD directly pinpoints the root-cause variables at the embedding level. OracleAD achieves state-of-the-art results across multiple real-world datasets and evaluation protocols, while remaining interpretable through SLS.

Abstract
Time series anomalies take diverse forms, and individual detectors encode different anomaly scoring principles that rarely cover all anomaly types. Recent unsupervised time-series anomaly detectors provide complementary scoring signals, yet deploying them as an ensemble preserves the inference and maintenance cost of every individual detector. We study whether heterogeneous teacher detectors can be distilled into a single lightweight detector in the unsupervised setting, where no anomaly labels are available to define a shared target. AnoMix addresses this by extracting weak pairwise supervision from the training-set anomaly scores of frozen teachers. It normalizes each teacher score sequence independently and trains the student with CoRank, a multi-teacher ranking-distillation objective that converts teacher score gaps into pairwise supervision through teacher consensus. Across seven benchmark datasets, AnoMix successfully integrates complementary teacher scoring principles into a single deployable model, improving over individual detectors and static score ensembles while using a compact student with under 80k parameters on the benchmark settings. A zero-shot variant further shows that the distilled ranking signal transfers to unseen datasets and can outperform large pretrained time-series foundation models for anomaly detection.

Abstract
When a language model develops capabilities not traceable to its individual components, when a multi-agent AI system outperforms each agent on its own, or when a human-AI pair succeeds where either fails in isolation, AI researchers describe it as a case of ``synergy'' or ``emergence''. However, these are usually informal assessments not supported by a theoretical framework, making these claims impossible to compare or even meaningfully dispute. Here we argue that \emph{information decomposition can serve as a unifying formal framework to operationalize assessments about synergy in AI systems}. Information decomposition provides a way to distinguish qualitatively different \emph{types} of information, including unique information available from individual components, redundant information shared across multiple components, and synergistic information accessible only from parts taken together. This gives claims of emergence a precise, falsifiable criterion: a system's behavior is emergent only if it carries synergistic information about task-relevant outcomes. We apply this lens to attention heads, autonomous agents, and human-AI teams, showing how it organizes existing knowledge across scales and substrates, while also generating cross-level conjectures that siloed fields would miss.

Abstract
Most algorithms for computing persistent homology do so by tracking cycles that represent homology classes. There are many choices of such cycles, and specific choices have found different uses in applications. Although it is known that persistence diagrams can be computed in matrix multiplication time [8] for the more general case of zigzag persistent homology, it is not clear how to extract cycle representatives, especially if specific representatives are desired. In this paper, we provide the same matrix multiplication bound for computing representatives for the two choices common in applications in the case of ordinary persistent (co)homology. We first provide a fast version of the reduction algorithm, which is simpler than the algorithm in [8], but returns a different set of representatives than the standard algorithm [6] We then give a fast version of a different variant called the row algorithm [4], which returns the same representatives as the standard algorithm.
Influence Is the Target-Response Jacobian Times Residuals
Authors: Paul Geertsema, Simon Geertsema

Comments: 19 pages, 2 figures, 8 tables

Abstract
We introduce the target-response Jacobian $J = [\partial \hat y_i/\partial y_j]$, which measures how model predictions respond to changes in individual training targets. $J$ complements training-data attribution (TDA) methods, which characterize sensitivity to the weights placed on training observations in the loss rather than to their targets, and can be computed matrix-free at the same cost as influence-based TDA methods. We show that sample-weight influence $\mathcal{I}$ and $J$ follow from the same implicit differentiation argument and differ only by a per-example multiplier: for pointwise losses, $\mathcal{I} = J\operatorname{diag}(m)$ exactly at any stationary point with an invertible Hessian. For canonical-link losses (squared error, logistic, Poisson) the multiplier is the training residual, $\mathcal{I} = J\operatorname{diag}(r)$, and softmax cross-entropy obeys a multiclass version. The identity predicts three ways in which influence fails when used in place of $J$: small residuals shrink its predicted shift towards zero (interpolation collapse), residuals of mixed sign wash out its direction (direction washout), and under softmax cross-entropy it collapses a training point's per-class sensitivities towards its own true class (true-class collapse). We score both operators against refitting with a per-prediction $R^2$ across target perturbations, with no rescaling. On tanh networks trained with squared error (Friedman #1) and logistic loss (MAGIC, HIGGS), with $N$ from 2,000 to $10^6$, $J$ reaches $R^2$ between 0.99 and 1.00, whereas influence's $R^2$ is negative on every dataset. On an MNIST softmax classifier, influence's $R^2$ is again negative, while $J$ reaches 0.92. On HIGGS, one row of $J$, holding all $10^6$ sensitivities of one prediction, takes under 0.2 seconds on an RTX 3090 after a one-time setup of under five minutes, whereas refitting would take $10^6$ refits.

Abstract
Time-series of persistence diagrams, more commonly known as vineyards, are a useful way to capture how multi-scale topological features vary over time. However, we do lose significant information in how the persistent homology at different time stamps relate to each other. A natural algebraic version of vineyards is a time series of persistence modules equipped with interleaving maps between the persistence modules at different time stamps. We call this a vineyard module. In this paper we will set up the framework for representing a vineyard module via an indexed set of vines alongside a collection of matrices. We outline an algorithmic way to transform the bases of the persistence modules at each time step within the vineyard module to make the matrices within this representation as simple as possible. With some reasonable assumptions on the vineyard modules, this simplified representation can be completely described (up to isomorphism) by the associated vineyard and a vector of finite length. While this vineyard and vector representation is not necessarily unique we can prove that the vector will always be the zero vector when the vineyard module is isomorphic to the direct sum of vine modules. We also show uniqueness when the vineyard module contains two vines (over field $\mathbb{F}_2$) and can characterise and count which are realisable, giving a complete account of isomorphism classes of vineyard modules with two vines. To define and analyse this approach we first must set up a lot of preliminary results about changes of bases for pairs of persistence modules where we are given $\varepsilon$-interleaving maps for sufficiently close $\varepsilon$. This new perspective on vineyards also provides a new approach to the study of multi-parameter persistence modules.

Abstract
Ranking data in modern applications often involves covariate vectors whose dimension grows with the number of objects. Identifying relevant covariates and integrating them with item-specific utilities can yield better predictive performance. In this work, we study a Plackett--Luce model that incorporates both item-specific utilities and high-dimensional covariate effects. Under a sparsity assumption on the covariate coefficients, we establish a necessary and sufficient condition for identifiability in terms of the Kruskal rank of the restricted covariate design. We then propose an $\ell_1$-regularized maximum likelihood estimator for the covariate coefficients. Under suitable design and connectivity conditions, we establish the existence and uniqueness of the estimator with probability tending to one, as well as its uniform consistency. With an additional minimum-signal condition, we further establish exact support recovery. Our analysis combines a hypergraph-based chaining technique with a profile-likelihood perturbation analysis, yielding estimation rates that explicitly capture the effects of the comparison hypergraph's connectivity and the underlying sparsity level. Numerical experiments using synthetic data and a horse racing dataset corroborate our theoretical findings.

Abstract
We introduce a clustering coefficient for nondirected and directed hypergraphs, which we call the quad clustering coefficient. We determine the average quad clustering coefficient and its distribution in real-world hypergraphs and compare its value with those of random hypergraphs drawn from the configuration model. We find that real-world hypergraphs exhibit a nonnegligible fraction of nodes with a maximal value of the quad clustering coefficient, while we do not find such nodes in random hypergraphs. Interestingly, these highly clustered nodes can have large degrees and can be incident to hyperedges of large cardinality. Moreover, highly clustered nodes are not observed in an analysis based on the pairwise clustering coefficient of the associated projected graph that has binary interactions, and hence higher order interactions are required to identify nodes with a large quad clustering coefficient.

Abstract
We address the problem of defining connected components in hypergraphs, which are models for systems with higher-order interactions. For graphs with dyadic interactions, connected components are defined in terms of paths connecting nodes along the graph. However, defining connected components in hypergraphs is a more involved problem, as one needs to consider the higher-order nature of the interactions associated with the hyperedge. Higher-order interactions can be taken into consideration through a logic associated with the hyperedges, two examples being OR-logic and AND-logic; these logical operations can be considered two limiting cases corresponding to non-cooperative and fully cooperative interactions, respectively. In this paper we show how connected components can be defined in hypergraphs with OR or AND logic. While OR-logic and AND-logic provide the same connected components for nondirected hypergraphs, for directed hypergraphs the strongly connected component of AND-logic is a subset of the OR-logic strongly connected component. Interestingly, higher-order interactions change the general topological properties of connected components in directed hypergraphs. Notably, while for directed graphs the strongly connected component is the intersection of its in- and out-component, in hypergraphs with AND-logic the intersection of in- and out-component does not equal the strongly connected component. We develop a theory for the fraction of nodes that are part of the largest connected component and through comparison with real-world data we show that degree-cardinality correlations play a significant role.

Abstract
People encounter infections through overlapping groups, such as households, classrooms, and workplaces. Understanding how these shared contacts affect the spread of multiple pathogens is critical to determining what information epidemic models need to retain. We introduce HyCo-SIS (Higher-order Co-infection SIS), a discrete-time SIS model on hypergraphs with explicit reinforcement between overlapping active groups and pairwise transmission. We investigate how this reinforcement affects infection dynamics and whether simplified representations of hypergraphs preserve the resulting behavior. We derive an exact representation of overlap reinforcement as target-dependent four-person interactions and determine when a symmetric representation is possible. Numerical experiments examine reinforcement strength, contact structure, and susceptibility to secondary infection, as well as calibrated pairwise approximations. Our results demonstrate that redistributing overlap weights can substantially change finite-time co-infection even when every individual's total overlap coefficient is preserved. We further derive an exact five-person calculation and demonstrate why equal mean infection pressure does not necessarily produce equal acquisition probabilities. These findings identify how the allocation of higher-order interactions can influence contagion dynamics beyond their aggregate strength. Beyond epidemiology, this work contributes to the statistical physics of spreading processes by examining how the arrangement of many-body interactions shapes collective dynamics, and what information is lost when those interactions are simplified.

Abstract
The persistent homology transform, Betti function transform, and Euler characteristic transform represent a shape with a multiset of persistence diagrams, Betti functions, or Euler characteristic functions, respectively, parameterized by the sphere of directions in the ambient space. In this work, we give the first explicit construction of finite sets of directions discretizing the verbose variants of these transforms and show that such discretizations faithfully represent the underlying shape. Our discretization, while exponential in the dimension of the shape, does not depend on any restrictions on the particular immersion beyond general position, and is stable with respect to various perturbations.

Abstract
Many complex systems involve group interactions, which can readily be represented by higher-order structures such as hypergraphs. However, when observing a system's dynamical evolution, establishing whether that evolution actually results from higher-order interactions, or whether pairwise interactions suffice to explain the data, remains a challenge.
